Chapter One

The Door Left Open

Maya had learned three things in her nineteen years.

Never let anyone know how much you need them.

Never ask questions you aren't prepared to hear the answers to.

And never, under any circumstances, let a wealthy person believe you've been listening to their private conversations.

She was about to break the last two rules.

The hardest part of living in America wasn't the work.

It wasn't being away from her family, although there were nights when she missed them so badly that she lay awake staring at the ceiling.

It wasn't even the strange feeling of being surrounded by things she couldn't afford, working in a house larger than the one her entire family now shared.

It was the English.

English at breakfast. English on television. English shouted between the children as they raced through the hallways. English at the grocery store, where cashiers spoke so quickly that Maya sometimes smiled and nodded without understanding a word.

Everyone seemed to be in a hurry to finish speaking.

In Oaxaca, people had always seemed to have time for conversation.

Here, Maya sometimes felt as though she was living behind a pane of glass, watching everyone else participate in a world she could see but couldn't quite enter.

Her mother had encouraged her to use the translation app on her phone.

Maya preferred watching people.

A raised eyebrow. A change in someone's voice. The way a person stood when they were irritated, or smiled when they wanted something.

Those things translated themselves.

She'd become very good at reading people.

She'd had to.

There had been a time when her family had money. Real money. The kind that meant her father wore beautiful watches and her mother hosted dinners where everyone seemed important.

Maya remembered polished floors, pretty dresses, and adults who spoke in low voices about business.

Then, almost overnight, everything changed.

She was young when it happened, but she remembered enough.

The bakery was gone.

The money was gone.

The people who used to visit their home stopped calling.

Her father became quieter. Her mother stopped wearing jewelry. Eventually, the entire family moved into her aunt's house.

Maya learned something important during those years.

People treated you differently when you needed them.

Now she was nineteen, living in America, and working as a caretaker and housekeeper for a family wealthy enough to employ people to do things her own mother once paid others to do.

Life had a strange sense of humor.

Still, she liked the family.

The children were wonderful, even when they were exhausting. Their mother was kind, and the house was beautiful.

And then there was the man of the house.

Maya tried not to think about him too much.

He was older than she was, considerably older, but handsome in a way she hadn't expected. He kept himself in excellent shape, with broad shoulders and the kind of quiet physical confidence that made him seem larger than he was.

He wasn't loud.

He didn't need to be.

When he entered a room, people noticed.

He rarely wasted words. When he spoke to Maya, he used simple English, sometimes accompanying a sentence with a gesture. He never seemed irritated when she struggled to understand.

And he never looked at her in a way that made her uncomfortable.

That was something she respected.

He was married. He had children. He appeared to love his family.

Maya found him attractive, certainly, but she had never mistaken attraction for an invitation.

Some men were handsome.

Some men were unavailable.

He happened to be both.

And as far as Maya was concerned, that was the end of it.

Until the afternoon she heard him shouting.

She was cleaning the hallway outside his office when she heard his voice.

At first, she barely noticed.

He was often on the phone. Business calls, she assumed. Sometimes he spoke for an hour, sometimes only a few minutes.

Usually, he was calm.

Today was different.

His voice was sharper than she'd ever heard it.

Maya slowed her movements.

The office door was open just a few inches.

She could hear him speaking English, but the words came too quickly for her to follow.

She caught fragments.

Something about a man.

Something about being certain.

Then silence.

She assumed whoever was on the other end was speaking.

The man answered, his voice rising.

And suddenly—

"¡Carajo, pensé que estaba muerto! ¡No hagas nada hasta que yo llegue!"

Damn it, I thought he was dead! Don’t do anything until I get there!

Maya froze.

Spanish.

He was speaking Spanish.

Not a few words learned on vacation. Not the awkward phrases Americans sometimes tried when they discovered she was Mexican.

Perfect Spanish.

Angry Spanish.

"Y por lo que más quieras, no le digas a nadie que sigue vivo."

And for the love of God, don’t tell anyone he’s still alive.

Maya stared at the door.

I told you he was dead.

Don't do anything until I get there.

Don't tell anyone he's still alive.

Her mind raced.

Who was dead?

Who was alive?

And why was the man who barely spoke more than a few words of English to her suddenly arguing in flawless Spanish?

She took a step toward the door.

Then another.

She knew she shouldn't.

Her mother would have been horrified.

Mind your business, Maya.

But curiosity had always been one of her weaknesses.

She moved close enough to see through the narrow opening.

He was standing beside his desk, phone pressed to his ear, one hand resting on his hip.

His expression was hard.

She'd never seen him look like that.

Then his eyes shifted toward the door.

Toward her.

Maya's stomach dropped.

"Okay. I'll call you back."

He ended the call.

For a moment, neither moved.

Then he opened the door.

"Maya?"

She stepped back immediately.

"Señor."

Sir.

He looked at the cleaning cloth in her hand, then at her face.

"How long have you been standing there?"

She recognized how long.

And standing.

But she couldn't assemble the rest quickly enough.

"No entiendo inglés, señor."

I don’t understand English, sir.

He studied her.

"Did you hear what I was saying?"

Maya shook her head.

"No entiendo."

I don’t understand.

His gaze remained on her a moment longer.

Then he nodded.

"Okay."

He stepped back into the office.

Maya turned and walked away, forcing herself not to hurry.

Only when she reached the laundry room did she realize how hard her heart was beating.

She closed the door.

He speaks Spanish.

That was the first thought.

The second was worse.

He knows I was listening.

Inside the office, he stood beside his desk with his phone still in his hand.

He replayed the moment.

The door.

Maya standing outside.

The look on her face.

He'd asked whether she'd understood.

She'd said she didn't understand English.

That was true enough. Her English was limited.

But the words he'd shouted hadn't been English.

He looked toward the hallway.

For several minutes, he considered calling her back.

Instead, he sat down.

His brothers had enough problems without him creating another.

And perhaps there was no problem.

Perhaps she'd heard nothing.

Perhaps she'd heard the Spanish but hadn't understood the significance.

He knew very little about the girl beyond what was necessary for her employment. She was young, hardworking, and unusually observant.

That last quality troubled him now.

He picked up his phone.

Set it down.

Then picked it up again.

By the time he left the office, he'd made a decision.

He found Maya folding towels.

"Maya."

She looked up immediately.

"Señor?"

Sir?

He held out his phone.

The translation app was open.

He spoke into it, waited for the Spanish translation to appear, and turned the screen toward her.

Did you understand anything you heard while I was on the phone?

Maya read it.

Her heart sank.

She shook her head.

"No, señor."

No, sir.

He watched her.

Then typed another question.

Are you sure?

"Sí, señor."

Yes, sir.

A pause.

He reached into his wallet.

Five hundred dollars.

He held the bills toward her.

Maya stared at the money.

She didn't reach for it.

He spoke into the translator again.

I would appreciate it if you kept what happened today private.

She looked from the phone to the bills.

Something cold moved through her stomach.

If she hadn't understood anything, why would he pay her to keep quiet?

And if he believed she hadn't understood anything, why was he asking?

She accepted the money because she didn't know how to refuse it.

"Gracias, señor."

Thank you, sir.

He nodded and walked away.

Halfway down the hallway, he realized what he'd done.

He had just handed five hundred dollars to a girl who claimed to have understood nothing.

If she'd been uncertain before, she certainly wasn't now.

He stopped.

For one brief moment, he considered turning around.

Then he continued walking.

Some mistakes became worse when you tried too hard to correct them.

The next two weeks were miserable.

Not openly.

Not in any way the rest of the family would have noticed.

The children still called for Maya. Their mother still asked her to help with meals and laundry. The household continued its familiar routines.

But whenever she encountered the man, Maya became conscious of things she'd never worried about before.

Was he watching her?

Had he noticed her hesitation?

Did he suspect she understood?

He behaved exactly as he always had.

That somehow made it worse.

She began avoiding his office.

She also found herself looking toward it more often.

Sometimes, when she was alone, she remembered the Spanish she'd heard through the door.

Te dije que estaba muerto.

No le digas a nadie que sigue vivo.

She tried not to think about what those words might mean.

She also couldn't stop wondering why a man who spoke Spanish so beautifully had never spoken a single word of it to her.

And then, one afternoon, everything changed again.

He had just finished exercising.

Maya was in the kitchen putting away clean dishes when he entered wearing workout clothes, a towel draped over one shoulder.

He poured himself a glass of water.

She smiled politely.

"Hello, señor."

"Hey, Maya."

He drank half the glass before setting it on the counter.

"Do you have family here?"

Maya turned from the cupboard.

"No, señor."

No, sir.

He nodded slightly.

"Friends? A boyfriend?"

She blinked.

"No..."

Why?

Why is he asking me this?

Whether she had a boyfriend was none of his business.

But she didn't dare say that.

Was he unhappy with her work? Was he thinking of sending her away? Had someone complained about her?

He watched her carefully, his expression giving nothing away.

There was something deliberate about his questions. Something about the way he waited for her answers that made her afraid to say anything more than necessary.

She lowered her eyes.

He took another sip of water.

Then he asked:

"¿Puedo confiar en ti?"

Can I trust you?

Maya stopped breathing.

For an instant, the question felt so natural that she almost answered.

Then she looked at him.

His Spanish was flawless.

And familiar.

Not the Spanish of the gardeners or the neighbors. Something older. Something from rooms filled with people her father had once known.

She stared at him.

"¿Por qué?"

Why?

The word escaped before she could stop it.

"No sé si tenías la edad suficiente para recordar cuando tu papá lo perdió todo," he said, ignoring her question.

I don’t know if you were old enough to remember when your father lost everything,

Maya's fingers tightened around the edge of the counter.

Her father.

How did he know about her father?

"Yo recuerdo," she said quietly. "Mi papá perdió nuestra panadería. En el dos mil diez. Toda la familia se mudó a la casa de mi tía después."

I remember, My dad lost our bakery. In 2010. The whole family moved into my aunt’s house afterward.

She watched his face.

He didn't appear surprised.

"Y seguro te estás preguntando por qué te pregunto esto y cómo sé lo de tu familia."

And I’m sure you’re wondering why I’m asking you this and how I know about your family.

"Sí," she whispered.

Yes,

Her mouth had gone dry.

She wanted to ask how he knew. She wanted to ask why he was speaking Spanish. She wanted to ask whether he'd understood every word she'd ever said in his house.

But she couldn't bring herself to ask any of them.

He seemed to know that.

"Mi familia también lo perdió todo. Los mismos políticos corruptos que le robaron la fortuna a tu familia también le robaron la nuestra. Nuestros padres se conocían muy bien y hacían negocios juntos. Solo recuerdo que mi padre me dijo que el tuyo tenía hijos pequeños."

My family lost everything too. The same corrupt politicians who stole your family’s fortune stole ours as well. Our fathers knew each other very well and did business together. I just remember my father telling me yours had young children.

Maya's lips parted.

"Nuestras familias... ¿se conocían?"

Our families... knew each other?

He nodded.

"Sí. Después de eso, mi padre se quitó la vida. Mi madre no pudo soportar vivir sin él y también se quitó la vida."

Yes. After that, my father took his own life. My mother couldn’t bear living without him, and she took her own life too.

He spoke quietly, without any noticeable change in his expression.

"Mis hermanos y yo nos quedamos solos. Mi hermano mayor tomó las riendas y se hizo cargo de la familia. Estaba lleno de rabia."

My brothers and I were left alone. My oldest brother took charge and looked after the family. He was full of rage.

Maya had forgotten about the dishes.

"Mis hermanos y yo encontramos a esos políticos y los matamos a todos."

My brothers and I found those politicians and killed every one of them.

She stared at him.

He continued.

"Huimos hasta aquí, aprendimos inglés e hicimos un pacto de no volver a hablar español jamás."

We fled here, learned English, and made a pact never to speak Spanish again.

Maya's hand dropped to the counter.

For a moment, she couldn't seem to move.

The man standing in front of her had just told her he'd killed people.

Not strangers from a story on the television.

Real people.

And he'd said it with the same quiet certainty he used when asking whether the laundry was finished.

"Yo... no sabía," she whispered.

I... didn’t know,

"No sabía quién eras hasta que hace un par de semanas le pedí a un viejo contacto de mi país que investigara tus antecedentes. Una vez que descubrí quién eras, me prometí a mí mismo que te mantendría a salvo."

I didn’t know who you were until a couple of weeks ago, when I asked an old contact from my country to look into your background. Once I found out who you were, I promised myself I would keep you safe.

Maya blinked.

He'd investigated her.

She remembered the envelope.

The five hundred dollars.

The translation app.

All those times she'd stood in the same room with him, speaking Spanish without giving a second thought to what he might understand.

She looked toward the kitchen doorway.

Then back at him.

"Ahora que lo sabes, el único peligro que podría surgir sería que alguien descubriera quién soy en realidad."

Now that you know, the only danger would be if someone discovered who I really am.

He paused.

"Así que nunca hablaremos español a menos que estemos completamente solos. Ni un solo error. Las consecuencias podrían ser mortales."

So we will never speak Spanish unless we are completely alone. Not one mistake. The consequences could be deadly.

Maya nodded quickly.

"Entiendo."

I understand.

She swallowed.

"No diré nada. Te lo prometo."

I won’t say anything. I promise.

For the first time since he'd entered the kitchen, something in his expression changed.

Not much.

Just enough.

He reached for her hand.

Maya hadn't expected him to touch her.

His fingers closed gently around hers.

"Además, quiero que sepas que estoy aquí para ti. Si alguna vez necesitas algo, lo que sea, absolutamente cualquier cosa, ven a mí."

Also, I want you to know I’m here for you. If you ever need anything—anything at all, absolutely anything—come to me.

Her heart began beating harder.

He was still holding her hand.

"Y espero que no te moleste que te lo diga, pero te has convertido en una joven preciosa."

And I hope you don’t mind me saying this, but you’ve grown into a beautiful young woman.

An unusual smile crossed his face.

Maya felt the warmth rise into her cheeks.

She couldn't seem to find her voice.

Preciosa.

She'd heard the word a thousand times.

Never quite like that.

She wondered whether he'd meant it the way it sounded.

Or whether she was being foolish.

After everything he'd just told her, how could she possibly be thinking about that?

"Espero no haberte ofendido," he said.

I hope I haven’t offended you,

"No, señor."

No, sir.

Her answer came too quickly.

She lowered her eyes, embarrassed by how eagerly she'd said it.

He released her hand.

For a moment, neither moved.

Then his expression softened again.

"Has pasado por mucho, Maya."

You’ve been through a lot, Maya.

She nodded.

"Y has tenido que enfrentarlo casi todo sola."

And you’ve had to face almost all of it alone.

Something tightened in her throat.

"Pero ya no tienes que hacerlo todo sola."

But you don’t have to do everything alone anymore.

He opened his arms.

"Ven aquí."

Come here.

Maya hesitated.

Then she stepped toward him.

His arms closed around her.

It should have been simple.

A kind man comforting a young woman after telling her something painful about their families.

Perhaps that was all it was.

But Maya felt the warmth of his body through his shirt, the solid pressure of his chest, the gentle weight of his hands against her back.

Her heart began beating faster.

She hoped he couldn't feel it.

"Estás a salvo conmigo," he said quietly. "Quiero que lo recuerdes."

You’re safe with me, I want you to remember that.

She nodded against his chest.

She should have stepped away.

Instead, for one small, selfish moment, she wished he would hold her a little longer.

He didn't tighten his embrace.

He didn't loosen it, either.

She wondered whether he could feel what was happening to her.

Whether he knew.

Whether the embrace meant something different to him than it did to her.

His breathing remained steady.

Finally, she stepped back.

He released her without hesitation.

For a moment, he studied her face. The warmth in his expression faded, replaced by the quiet seriousness she'd come to recognize.

"No español."

No Spanish.

Two words. Firm. Absolute.

Maya nodded.

"Sí, señor."

Yes, sir.

He picked up his glass and started toward the hallway.

At the doorway, he glanced back.

"Try not to fall in love with me, Maya."

The faintest smirk touched one corner of his mouth.

He didn't wait for an answer.

Then he was gone.

Maya stared after him.

She'd caught her name. Nothing else.

What just happened?

He always spoke slowly to her. Carefully. Sometimes repeating himself or using his hands when she didn't understand.

But not that time.

That time he'd spoken quickly, almost casually, with that little smile she'd never seen before.

Was he making fun of me?

She wasn't sure.

She wasn't even sure she wanted to know.

But as she stood there, staring at the empty doorway, one thing became perfectly clear.

She was going to find out exactly what he'd said.

Chapter Two

Michael Bennett, the oldest of three brothers, understood the meaning of sacrifice in a way few people ever would. Some debts, he'd learned, could never be repaid.

Michael lived a simple life. His small studio apartment sat above an auto repair shop. He turned wrenches downstairs during the day and spent most nights staring at a television, never hearing a word that came out of it.

Like most men, Michael had his strengths and weaknesses. He knew exactly how many steps it took to reach the supermarket and could tell if the mailman was an hour late, but couldn't always tell you what day of the week it was.

"Yeah, for a 2018 Toyota Camry. Plugs and a filter. Yeah. No, that's perfect. Thanks, Doug. See ya in a bit."

Over the years, Michael had grown fond of Doug. Doug was a polite, busy man who rarely had time for small talk.

A couple of hours later, Michael stood with his head beneath the hood of the Camry. Without looking, he reached for his spark plug socket, loosened and removed the four spark plugs, and returned the socket to its place without ever taking his eyes off his work.

"Right on time," Michael said to himself as he heard tires crunching over the gravel outside.

Doug moved like clockwork.

As footsteps approached, Michael didn't look up.

"Just leave it on the counter, Doug. Thanks."

No answer.

No box hitting the counter.

"Rafa."

Michael's heart skipped once, but his expression didn't change.

He turned, looking beyond the man and instinctively checking his surroundings before uttering a word of Spanish. He kept one arm behind his back, his hand gripping the revolver at his waist.

Michael recognized the voice immediately.

The voice of a man who had once begged for his life.

He studied the man's face for a moment, making certain it was him. He remembered everything. The face. The voice. The gunshot.

Even the scar that stretched from his ruined eye toward his ear.

"Tuviste suerte de perder solo un ojo."

You were lucky you only lost an eye.

"Tranquilo, Rafa. Si quisiera verte muerto, no estaríamos hablando."

Easy, Rafa. If I wanted you dead, we wouldn't be talking.

Michael tightened his grip on the revolver and slowly pulled back the hammer.

Click.

Don Amado's remaining eye flicked toward the sound.

"Ya tuviste tu oportunidad, Rafa. Pero hoy no va a morir ninguno de los dos. Vengo de parte de tu jefe."

You already had your chance, Rafa. But neither of us is dying today. I'm here on behalf of your boss.

Don Amado tossed a folder onto the counter.

"Supongo que no tengo que explicarte lo que debes hacer. Me han contado cuál es tu papel en la organización."

I assume I don't have to explain what you need to do. I've been told about your role in the organization.

"Acabé con tu organización. ¿Y ahora eres la perra de Pablo?"

I wiped out your organization. And now you're Pablo's bitch?

Don Amado smiled, the kind of smile only a man who had been brought back from the dead and given a chance at revenge could manage.

"Haz tu maldito trabajo."

Do your fucking job.

For a moment, the anger slipped through. Then his smile returned.

"O la próxima vez que venga, será para terminar lo que tú empezaste."

Or the next time I come, it'll be to finish what you started.

He turned and walked toward his car.

Doug nodded at Don Amado as they passed each other.

"You want them on the counter?" Doug asked.

"Sure, Doug. Thanks."

Doug left as quickly as he'd arrived.

Michael sat down with the folder.

He opened it.

A business card. A photograph.

He picked up the photograph and stared.

His jaw tightened.

Then he stood so abruptly that the chair slid backward across the floor.

Chapter Three

Maya had just finished folding the clothes. On her way back to the laundry room, she noticed the light in Adrian’s office was still on.

Just keep walking, Maya. That is none of your business. Remember what happened last time?

She kept walking and returned the empty basket.

Where are you going? Your room is the other way. Turn around. Right now.

She ignored herself.

Instead, she walked past Adrian’s office one more time, hoping to overhear something—or better yet, that he might come out and talk to her.

It wasn't that Adrian had been talking to her any less than before. She just wanted to talk to him more.

She had questions.

And she wanted another hug.

Stop thinking like that. Just do your job. He's happily married, and they pay you way more than you deserve.

But no matter how many times she told herself to forget it, she couldn't ignore how safe she'd felt during those brief moments in his arms.

—

She changed into her pajamas, brushed her hair, and settled onto her bed with her legs propped up, scrolling through her phone.

Mom had texted something about the family dog chewing up an heirloom. Another friend insisted she needed to talk because she'd just met the love of her life.

For the third time this year.

Maya laughed to herself.

Just look. It's no big deal.

She opened her photos, scrolled up a little, and found the picture of Adrian playing outside with the kids.

Except now, the kids were almost entirely cropped out.

It was just Adrian, smiling back at her.

She smiled, turned off her phone, and slipped beneath the covers, leaving it beside her.

A moment later, it began to vibrate.

Mom? At this hour?

“¿Bueno, mamá?”

Hello, Mom?

“Mija, escúchame.”

Sweetheart, listen to me.

There was a fear in her mother's voice that Maya had never heard before.

A chill ran down her spine.

“¿Mamá? ¿Qué pasa?” she asked, already close to tears.

Mom? What's wrong?

“Mija... no regreses a México. Ahora las tierras son tuyas.”

Sweetheart... don't come back to Mexico. The land is yours now.

“¿Qué pasó? ¡Mamá! ¿De qué estás hablando...?”

What happened? Mom! What are you talking about...?

“¿CON QUIÉN ESTÁS HABLANDO? ¿QUIÉN ES? ¡CONTESTA!”

WHO ARE YOU TALKING TO? WHO IS IT? ANSWER ME!

A man's voice was screaming in the background, and her mother cried out.

The line went dead.

Maya immediately called back.

Nothing.

She was crying uncontrollably now. She tried again.

Nothing.

She called her father's phone.

Nothing.

WHAT IS GOING ON?

She scrolled back to her mother's number and pressed call again.

This time, someone answered.

“¿Quién habla?”

Who's this?

Maya froze.

She didn't know what to do. She was terrified, but she needed to know what was happening.

She hung up.

She wasn't sure why she'd done it, but something told her not to give this man anything. Not even her name.

Something terrible had just happened.

And whoever had answered her mother's phone was involved.

Maya stared at the phone, trying to make sense of what she'd just heard.

Her mother's voice. The man shouting. That final cry.

She couldn't stop crying.

She had never felt so alone.

Then came a knock at the door.

“Maya? Are you okay?”

Adrian.

Chapter Four

The Price

Maya opened the door.

The moment she saw Adrian, she broke down.

She tried to speak, but another sob took the place of her words. She stepped forward and threw her arms around him.

Adrian held her.

“Maya, I'm here.”

She buried her face against his chest, sobbing uncontrollably.

Her mother's voice kept playing in her head. The shouting. That final cry. The silence afterward.

She wanted to call again. She wanted her mother to answer. She wanted to hear her father in the background complaining that she was keeping her mother on the phone too long.

Instead, she had heard a stranger.

Adrian said nothing more. He just held her while she cried.

When she finally managed to speak, the words came in broken Spanish.

“Mi mamá... algo pasó. No sé qué pasó.”

My mom... something happened. I don't know what happened.

“Tell me.”

She tried to explain. Her mother had called from Oaxaca. She had sounded frightened. She had told Maya not to come home. She had said the family's land belonged to Maya now.

Then there had been a man's voice.

Her mother had cried out.

And the call had ended.

“I called back,” Maya said, struggling through the English. “Many times. A man answered. Not my papá.”

“Did you speak to him?”

She shook her head.

“Did he say anything?”

“¿Quién habla?”

Who's speaking?

Adrian watched her carefully.

“Did you tell him your name?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Maya wiped her face, but the tears kept coming.

“I don't know if they're okay.”

“I know.”

“I need to go home.”

Adrian didn't answer immediately.

She looked up at him.

“Please. I need my mamá.”

He understood what she was asking. He also understood that she was frightened enough to get on a plane without knowing what she might be flying into.

“We're going to find out what happened,” he said. “But you're not going back until we know it's safe.”

She shook her head.

“My mamá—”

“I know, Maya.”

He didn't tell her everything would be fine. He had no way of knowing that.

“Get your phone,” he said. “And whatever you need for tonight.”

“Where we go?”

“To see someone who might be able to help.”

—

Adrian waited downstairs while Maya gathered her things.

He took out his phone and called Catherine.

“Hey,” she answered.

“Something came up with Maya's family. I'm taking her to the airport.”

“Is everything all right?”

“I'm not sure yet.”

“Is she going back to Mexico?”

“That's what we're trying to figure out.”

“All right. Let me know if you need anything.”

“I will.”

He ended the call.

For a moment, he stared at the phone.

Why the hell did I just lie to her?

He slipped it into his pocket.

A few minutes later, Maya came downstairs carrying a small bag. Her eyes were swollen, and she held her phone tightly in one hand.

“Ready?” he asked.

She nodded.

He opened the door for her.

—

Michael was in the garage when they arrived.

He looked up from the workbench as Adrian walked in, then glanced at Maya standing beside him.

“Who's this?”

“This is Maya. She works for us.”

Michael wiped his hands on a rag.

“Hola, Maya.”

Hello, Maya.

“Hola, señor.”

Hello, sir.

“Michael,” he corrected gently. “Just Michael.”

Adrian glanced toward the office.

“We need to talk.”

Michael looked from his brother to Maya.

“About what?”

“Don Amado.”

The expression on Michael's face changed.

He tossed the rag onto the bench.

“Come inside.”

Michael led them into his small office. He pulled out a chair for Maya, then reached into a cabinet for a bottle of water.

“Here.”

“Gracias.”

Thank you.

Adrian waited until she was seated.

“Her family's in Oaxaca,” he told Michael. “Something happened today. She was on the phone with her mother when a man started shouting. The call ended, and now she can't reach either of her parents.”

Michael looked at Maya.

“What part of Oaxaca?”

She told him.

“Did your mother say what was happening?”

Maya shook her head.

“She say don't come home. And...” She swallowed. “The land is mine now.”

Michael's eyes narrowed slightly.

“The land?”

“Sí. De mi familia.”

Yes. My family's.

“Did she say why?”

“No.”

Michael leaned back.

“Do you have anyone else there? An aunt? Uncle? Somebody you trust?”

Maya thought for a moment and gave him a name.

Michael took a piece of paper from the desk.

“Write it down. And a phone number if you have one.”

She did.

“I know a few people who might be able to make some calls,” Michael said. “I can't promise anything, but we'll see what we can find out.”

“Gracias,” Maya whispered.

Thank you.

Michael nodded.

Adrian looked at him.

“Can we have a minute?”

Michael glanced at Maya, then toward the garage.

“There's a little waiting room out front. You can sit there if you'd like. Nobody will bother you.”

Maya looked at Adrian.

“I'll be right here,” he told her.

She took her water and phone and stepped out.

Michael waited until the door closed.

Then he looked at his brother.

“What the hell is going on?”

“That's what I'm trying to figure out.”

“No. I mean why did you bring her here?”

“Because she's scared, Michael. She doesn't know whether her parents are alive, and somebody she doesn't know answered her mother's phone.”

“And you brought her to me?”

“You know people in Mexico.”

Michael studied him for a moment.

“Fine. I'll see what I can do.”

Adrian nodded.

“Now let's talk about Don Amado.”

Michael's expression hardened.

“What about him?”

“You told me he was alive.”

“He is.”

“You're certain?”

“Adrian, the son of a bitch was standing in my garage.”

Adrian looked toward the door.

“Where is he now?”

“I don't know.”

“Then we find him.”

“No.”

Adrian looked back at him.

“What?”

“I said no.”

Michael walked behind his desk.

Adrian followed him with his eyes.

“He killed our fucking parents, Michael.”

“I remember.”

“Then what are we talking about? He's alive. We find him, and we finish what we started.”

“No, we don't.”

Adrian stared at him.

“You called me and told me you almost shot him in the face. Now you're telling me to leave him alone?”

“That's exactly what I'm telling you.”

“Why?”

“Because I said so.”

Adrian gave a short, disbelieving laugh.

“Because you said so?”

“Don't start with me.”

“I'm not fifteen anymore, Michael.”

“I know how old you are.”

“Then stop talking to me like I'm a fucking kid.”

Michael stood.

“I said NO, goddamn it! I make the decisions!”

Adrian stepped closer to the desk.

“You did when I was fifteen, hermano. We're past that.”

Adrian turned and opened the office door to leave.

“¡ANDRÉS!”

Michael's voice thundered through the office.

Adrian froze.

For an instant, he was fifteen again. He glanced toward the waiting room, where Maya sat staring at him with wide, questioning eyes.

He slowly turned back and closed the office door.

He looked at his brother with the same mixture of fear and respect he'd felt after their parents died. But this time, there was something else in his expression.

You'd better give me some goddamn answers.

For a moment, neither brother spoke.

Michael looked at Adrian, and something in his expression changed. The anger was still there, but there was something underneath it. A different kind of pain, and Michael was no stranger to pain.

He remembered a younger Adrian who had never questioned whether his brother would keep him alive. A boy who had followed him out of Mexico because Michael told him they were going to make it.

Now that boy was standing in front of him, questioning the one thing Michael had never stopped doing.

Michael opened his desk drawer.

He pulled out a photograph and slammed it onto the desk.

“THAT'S WHY!”

Adrian looked down.

He recognized the picture immediately.

Catherine.

His children.

Himself.

For several seconds, he didn't move.

Then he picked it up.

“Where the fuck did you get this?”

“Don Amado.”

Adrian looked at his brother.

“He brought this here?”

“Yesterday. Right into my garage.”

Adrian looked down again.

It wasn't an old photograph. He recognized the clothes. He remembered the day it had been taken.

Someone knew where his family was.

Someone had found him.

Someone had been close enough to photograph them.

“Who else has seen this?”

“I don't know.”

“Who took it?”

“I don't know that either.”

Adrian set the photograph down.

“Then we find Amado and make him tell us.”

Michael shook his head.

“Jesus Christ, Adrian. Are you even listening to me? Don Amado is a fucking messenger now. Don Pablo is calling the shots. If you kill Amado, Pablo sends a fucking army, and then I really can't protect you.”

“I'm looking at a photograph of my wife and children that the man who murdered our parents brought into your garage. What the fuck do you expect me to do? And what do you mean Pablo is calling the shots now? How the fuck do you know so much about Pablo?”

“Mira,” Michael said, his voice lower now.

For years, he'd imagined telling his brothers the truth. Sometimes he'd rehearsed the words in his head while driving home.

“Who do you think got us across that border?”

Adrian stared at him.

“You did, hermano.”

Michael snorted. Not because he found it funny, but because his brother still had no idea.

“Three Mexican boys with no money don't get to wake up one day and decide to go live in America, Adrian. You don't get to forget about your past, marry a beautiful woman, and raise a family. Ernesto doesn't get to pick up and start over. Not for free.”

Adrian said nothing.

“Deals have to be made. Deals with people you don't ever want to make deals with.”

“What deals?”

Michael looked at his brother.

“I remember a time when you believed in me. When you knew that the only thing I lived for was protecting you.”

“I still believe in you.”

“Then why the fuck are you standing here questioning me?”

“Because you won't tell me what's going on!”

Michael took a moment before answering.

“Don Pablo got us into this country. He gave you and Ernesto new identities. New lives. Everything you have today.”

Adrian looked down at the photograph.

“And what did he get?”

Michael didn't answer immediately.

He looked at Catherine, at the children, at the life his brother had built.

Then he tapped the photograph.

“And I paid for it.”
